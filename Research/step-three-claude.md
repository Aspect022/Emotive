## 1. Scalogram Stacking

### 1.1 Mathematical foundation

A scalogram is the time–scale energy representation obtained from the **continuous wavelet transform (CWT)**. For a signal x(t), the CWT is

W(s,τ) = ∫ x(t) · ψ*ₛ,τ(t) dt, where ψₛ,τ(t) = (1/√s) ψ((t−τ)/s)

with s the scale (inversely related to frequency) and τ the time shift. The scalogram is the absolute value of the CWT coefficients, structured as a 3D tensor whose layers each correspond to convolution of the signal with a different-scale mother wavelet, and the corresponding wavelet energy distribution is E(fₛ) = (1/T)∫|W(t,fₛ)|² dt — formally analogous to a Fourier power spectrum but built from wavelet coefficients rather than STFT bins.

The near-universal mother wavelet for EEG/MEG scalograms is the **complex Morlet wavelet**, a sine wave tapered by a Gaussian: w(t,f) = e^(2iπft)·e^(−t²/2σ²), where σ controls the Gaussian width and the time–frequency precision trade-off is governed by the "number of cycles" parameter n. For neurophysiological data (EEG, MEG, LFP), typical values of n range from 2 to 15 across frequencies of 2–80 Hz, and a fixed value around 5 cycles is commonly used in EEG analysis as a good compromise between temporal and spectral resolution. Cohen's reformulation in terms of full-width-at-half-maximum (rather than raw cycle count) is worth adopting directly, since it makes the smoothing choice interpretable in both domains simultaneously rather than leaving it as an opaque, hard-to-report parameter.

**Direct implication for your hardware constraint:** a 1-second sub-window at 128 Hz gives you only 128 samples per channel per window. Given your 0.5–45 Hz bandpass, a CWT computed at low frequencies (e.g., delta, 0.5–4 Hz) inside a 1-second window is going to be severely edge-affected — you won't get a clean full cycle of a 1 Hz oscillation in a 1-second frame. This is a real constraint scalogram-based approaches will inherit that PSD/DE approaches partially sidestep via longer averaging windows; it needs to be either addressed (widen the analysis window with padding/overlap-and-discard at the CWT stage even if the label unit stays 1 s) or explicitly flagged as a limitation in any report claiming this technique.

### 1.2 Scalogram-CNN literature (single/spatial-only representations)

Scalogram-based CNN classification has strong precedent across biosignal domains, generally outperforming raw 1D or hand-crafted-feature baselines when enough data exists:

- A wavelet-scalogram-infused deep CNN classifier identified post-hypoxic EEG spike transients with 99.81 ± 0.15% accuracy (AUC = 1.000) across 5,010 annotated EEG waveforms in neonatal-model data.
- Scalogram-based CNN processing of raw EEG has been reported to outperform comparator methods for epileptic seizure classification (Türk & Özerdem).
- Sadam & Nalini's SD-CNN combined CWT-derived scalograms with an SVM stage for seizure detection, reaching roughly 94% accuracy (Signal, Image and Video Processing, 2024).
- A hybrid Swin Transformer + Temporal Convolutional Network operating on CWT scalograms improved emotion-recognition accuracy on the SEED dataset by up to 6.7% over recent CNN/RNN/Transformer baselines.
- A CNN-SVM pipeline over 2D scalograms reached 98.33% accuracy on the DROZY drowsiness dataset, outperforming both 1D CNNs on raw signal and transfer-learned VGG16/ResNet50, specifically framed around reducing inter-subject variability — directly relevant to your subject-dependent setup.
- For a different biosignal (PPG), 224×224×3 scalograms with a Cgau1 wavelet and 300-sample segments reached 90% blood-pressure classification accuracy without evident overfitting, illustrating the wavelet-family/segment-length sensitivity you'd need to tune empirically rather than assume.
- Chaudhary et al. (2022) used a 2D-CNN-scalogram AlexNet variant specifically for EEG mental-task classification — the closest direct precedent to your "5 cognitive tasks" framing, though on motor-imagery-style skull-mapped inputs rather than your task battery.
- Concatenated dual-input GoogLeNet architectures that take both scalogram and spectrogram representations simultaneously outperformed single-representation AlexNet/GoogLeNet/ResNet-18/ResNet-50 baselines — an easy ablation to run given you already compute PSD (spectrogram-adjacent) features.

### 1.3 The stacking mechanisms (this is the actual technique you're asking about)

"Stacking" in this literature means treating each channel's (or each frequency-band's) scalogram as one layer of a multi-channel image tensor, analogous to RGB channels, then feeding the whole cuboid into a 2D or 3D CNN. Each feature map is resized to a common spatial resolution and concatenated along a new channel axis — this generic recipe recurs with variations across at least five concrete implementations worth citing directly:

1. **Row-wise multichannel stacking.** Aarotale & Rattani computed a per-second time-frequency scalogram for each of 12 ECG leads and stacked them row-wise into a single composite image, which was then fed to pretrained CNN, 1D-CNN, and 1D-CNN-LSTM models, reaching about 98% test accuracy with the fine-tuned CNN and 95% with CNN-LSTM. This is the most literal analog of stacking your 14 EPOC+ channels into one composite scalogram image.

2. **Cuboid fusion (Z/V-scalogram).** Multidomain CNN models for ERP classification fuse multichannel Z-scalograms (artifact coefficients outside the cone of influence zeroed out) into a frequency-time-spatial cuboid, exploiting frequency, time, and spatial (electrode-position) information jointly rather than training single-channel classifiers per electrode — directly useful since your 14-channel layout has a defined 10-20-style spatial arrangement you could exploit the same way.

3. **Channel-attention stacking.** SCLE-2D-CNN extracts time-frequency features from multichannel EEG by stacking scaled convolutional layers channel-by-channel, then applies a channel-enhancement module that reassigns learned importance weights to each physical electrode before the 2D-CNN classification stage, evaluated on the GAMEEMO dataset. This addresses a real problem for your 14-channel EPOC+ layout: some electrodes (e.g., frontal AF3/AF4/F7/F8) are likely to carry more task-discriminative signal for arithmetic/attention tasks than occipital ones (O1/O2), and a fixed unweighted stack wastes capacity.

4. **Scale-selected multi-frame stacking.** A convolutional-recurrent hybrid converted each of 32 EEG channels into a scalogram using wavelet coefficients selected in the scale range 7–38 (chosen via an Energy-Entropy Ratio criterion), then encapsulated the 32-channel scalograms into multi-time-step frames fed sequentially into a CNN (for cross-channel correlation) followed by an LSTM (for temporal context). The explicit scale-selection step (rather than using the full CWT scale range indiscriminately) is a detail most re-implementations skip and shouldn't.

5. **3D stacking over consecutive segments.** A separate 3D-CNN approach for EEG emotion detection constructed its input by stacking multichannel Hilbert-spectrum representations over consecutive signal segments rather than channels alone — i.e., stacking along a *temporal* rather than purely spatial axis, which given your 1-second sliding sub-windows could be a natural way to reintroduce inter-window temporal context that a 1-second-window classifier otherwise discards.

6. **Fusion-strategy ablation.** A motor-imagery CWT-scalogram study isolated the CNN fusion mechanism itself, comparing summation vs. concatenation fusion of GoogleNet/AlexNet/ResNet50 features, finding concatenation-based fusion reached 98.45% accuracy and 97.94% kappa versus lower results for summation on BCI Competition IV 2a — a useful ablation template if you end up comparing stacking-then-single-CNN against parallel-CNN-then-fusion architectures.

A relevant systematic-review framing: across the cognitive-workload EEG literature, DNN input formulations cluster into feature vectors, single/multi-channel image matrices, and raw EEG matrices, with feature vectors still the most common choice and multi-channel image (stacked-scalogram-style) inputs a growing but still minority approach — meaning you'd be moving from the dominant paradigm (your current 252 handcrafted PSD/DE features) into a less-traveled but demonstrably viable one.

**Concrete recommendation for CogProfile-Net:** given 14 channels × 4 bands of interest × 1-second windows, a 14-channel-deep cuboid (mechanism #2/#3 above) sized roughly 64×64×14 (frequency bins × time bins × channels) fed to a moderately shallow 2D-CNN (not a full ResNet-50 — you don't have the data volume) is the most defensible starting point, with channel-attention (mechanism #3) added once you've confirmed baseline stacked performance. Given only ~25 subjects, expect scalogram-CNN approaches to need aggressive regularization and are the primary place digital-twin-style synthetic augmentation (Section 2) earns its keep.

## 2. Digital Twin Integration

### 2.1 What "digital twin" actually means in this literature (definitional grounding)

This term is used at wildly different scales in the literature, and conflating them will make any report look uninformed, so the taxonomy matters. The most rigorous recent formalization: a virtual brain twin is built from multimodal data D obtained from a human brain — anatomical (T1-MRI, diffusion-weighted MRI, CT, PET) and functional (EEG, MEG, stereo-EEG, fMRI) — combined into a personalized network model, with the core idea being a personalized digital replica built by combining structural connectivity data with functional activity data to simulate how brain regions communicate, explicitly named as a future avenue for guiding brain-computer-interface development. The "Digital Twin Brain" framing more generally comprises three elements: the structural scaffold that grounds the twinning process, bottom-layer generative models of brain function, and a downstream layer of applications — including simulating cognitive tasks, not just resting-state activity.

At the most computationally extreme end: Lu et al.'s 2022/2023 DTB work built a framework simulating up to 86 billion neurons using 3,500+ compute nodes and 10,000+ GPUs, with a spiking neural circuit model at single-neuron resolution across 378 cortical/subcortical regions (HCPex parcellation) totaling 9.45 million neurons, fit via a diffusion hierarchical mesoscale data-assimilation method. This tier is irrelevant to you — it requires fMRI/DWI connectome data and supercomputer-scale resources you don't have and don't need.

The far more relevant tier is EEG-native, subject-specific, data-assimilation-based digital twins, several of which are recent (2024–2026) and directly transferable in spirit to a 14-channel consumer setup:

- A two-stage digital-twin-brain (TS-DTB) jointly constrained by EEG and fMRI captured subject-specific multi-scale brain dynamics in both healthy and Alzheimer's cohorts, mechanistically linking cognitive decline to excitation-inhibition imbalance and simulating rTMS intervention responses — the paper's own framing is instructive: individualized forward modeling, not generic population-level simulation, is what converts a model into a genuine subject-specific "digital twin".
- A real-time ECoG-based digital twin used a Variational Bayesian Recurrent Neural Network with hierarchical latent units to assimilate broad-band signal from macaques across awake/anesthetized states, dynamically updating latent brain-state estimates in real time and enabling virtual-intervention simulation (e.g., simulated drug administration) via a free-energy-principle / Bayesian-brain data-assimilation objective. This is architecturally the closest match to what's feasible on your data: a recurrent latent-state model continuously updated from streaming signal, rather than a full connectome simulation.
- An EEG-fused digital twin brain for autonomous driving used a Bayesian inference framework establishing voxel-wise mappings between millisecond-scale EEG and structural-MRI-derived spiking networks, achieving 0.88 resting-state and 0.60 task-state correlation between simulated and real signals, with downstream steering-angle decoding outperforming both chance and real-signal baselines in closed-loop simulation.
- BrainTwin-AI, an MRI-EEG cognitive digital twin, used a BiLSTM classifier over EEG spectral features to distinguish relaxed/stress/fatigue states framed explicitly as "probabilistically inferred cognitive conditions," fusing this with structural embeddings from a vision transformer to output a single interpretable brain-health risk score with Grad-CAM explainability — structurally very close to your "calibrated cognitive profile (probabilistic, not hard label)" pipeline stage, just applied to a health-risk output rather than a career-aptitude output.
- A scalable IoT-integrated cognitive digital twin architecture used a wearable EEG skullcap feeding edge-level preprocessing (Raspberry Pi) and cloud-based digital-twin simulation, reporting 94.6% precision / 93.2% recall on a downstream classification task — a useful reference architecture if any part of CogProfile-Net is meant to run outside a lab (edge preprocessing → cloud twin update loop).
- One framing worth adopting directly: digital twins of the brain are conceptually inseparable from generative AI, since a generative model is what supplies the interpretable, mechanistic, resampleable signature that a discriminative classifier alone cannot provide — this is the bridge to Section 2.2 below.

A systematic review specifically warns on generalizability: deep architectures applied to biomarker-based cognitive digital twins carry real overfitting risk on the small datasets typical of neuropsychological research (the review's own median across studies was N=127), and real-world deployment performance is likely to run 10–15% below published validation figures. ### 2.2 What "digital twin" can concretely mean for CogProfile-Net (given your actual constraints)

A full Virtual-Brain-Twin (connectome + fMRI/DWI) is off the table — you have no structural imaging and 14 surface electrodes. The honest, useful scope is a **shallow personalized generative/state-space twin**, with two complementary components:

**(a) A subject-specific generative model as a synthetic-data engine.** This directly addresses your 25-subject constraint. This is a well-established pattern independent of the "digital twin" label:
- EGAN, an ensemble adversarial network purpose-built for cognitive-load-level prediction, generates multichannel EEG recordings jointly with their spatial-spectral representation, explicitly designed to preserve topological structure and manage the bias-variance trade-off that arises from limited-subject EEG datasets — this is essentially a "digital twin as data generator" already implemented for a cognitive-load task adjacent to yours.
- A DCGAN-GP variant trained on 2D time-frequency maps (rather than raw signal) improved motor-imagery classifier robustness across subjects on BCI IV 2b — notably, this generates *scalogram-like* representations, meaning techniques 1 and 2 in your prompt could share a generative-augmentation stage rather than being developed independently.
- A review of EEG data-augmentation methods notes that PSD and differential-entropy representations (i.e., your existing 252 features) are well-suited to GAN-based artificial-sample generation, and separately flags that extracting representations that generalize across subjects and sessions was identified early on (Bashivan et al., 2015) as the central obstacle in EEG-based cognitive-state modeling — which is precisely the obstacle a per-subject twin is meant to address by *not* trying to generalize across subjects at the representation level, but instead maintaining one twin per subject.
- Standard generative-AI options for this purpose are GANs, VAEs, and denoising diffusion probabilistic models; given your dataset size, a VAE is likely the more stable choice than a GAN (fewer failure modes, no adversarial training instability), consistent with schizophrenia-diagnosis work that found VAEs required substantially less computational overhead per iteration than a WGAN counterpart while remaining effective for augmentation.

**(b) A per-subject latent state-space model, updated online, that the classifier and the calibration stage both read from.** This is the part that most resembles the "twin" framing in the primate-ECoG and driving papers above: rather than a stateless per-window classifier, maintain a low-dimensional latent cognitive-state vector zₜ per subject, updated at each 1-second window via something structurally like a Kalman-filter or variational-RNN update (zₜ | zₜ₋₁, xₜ), where xₜ includes both your EEG features and the behavioral metadata you already have per trial — reaction time, difficulty level, accuracy. This does two things your current architecture doesn't: (i) it lets adjacent sub-windows within a 4-second trial inform each other rather than being classified independently (partially answering the leakage question in Section 4, since if temporal dependence is *modeled* rather than *ignored*, it stops being a hidden confound), and (ii) it gives the calibration stage a natural probabilistic object to calibrate — a filtered state estimate rather than a raw classifier score.

## 3. Clustering — the connective layer

### 3.1 Formal grounding

The canonical objective, k-means: J(θ,U) = Σᵢ Σⱼ uᵢⱼ‖xᵢ − θⱼ‖², where θⱼ is the j-th cluster centroid and uᵢⱼ = 1 if point i is closest to centroid j (0 otherwise), iterated until convergence. The standard way to choose cluster count k is the elbow/knee method: running k-means across a range of k values and identifying where the plot of minimized J against k shows a sharp bend — the same formulation has been applied directly to EEG phase-synchrony patterns to discover discrete "synchrostates" during a cognitive (face-perception) task. A patent covering individual-level cognitive-trait estimation instead used the Xie-Beni index, sweeping k from 2–10, to determine the optimal number of compact, well-separated clusters per subject — worth considering as an alternative to the elbow method if your per-subject cluster structure turns out to be less obviously "elbow-shaped."

### 3.2 Where clustering earns its place in your pipeline

Four distinct, non-overlapping uses are supported by the literature, and I'd recommend distinguishing them explicitly rather than treating "clustering" as one monolithic addition:

**Sub-state discovery within your five task labels.** Your five classes are experimental-condition labels, not verified ground-truth cognitive states — a subject nominally doing "sustained attention" is not attending uniformly for the full 4 seconds. K-means and Gaussian Mixture Models can group EEG segments by intrinsic feature similarity to reveal brain states that a fixed labeling scheme doesn't capture, and a fully unsupervised PCA→UMAP→K-means pipeline requiring no manual annotation successfully recovered physiologically meaningful microstates in an EEG classification context, with cluster count selected via elbow and silhouette methods together. Applied to your 37,804 sub-windows, this could reveal (for instance) that "Sustained Attention" sub-windows actually split into 2–3 latent engagement clusters — directly useful for the "calibrated, probabilistic, not hard label" framing already in your pipeline, since a soft cluster-membership vector is a more honest probabilistic target than a hard 5-way label to begin with.

**Handling label uncertainty directly.** One study used unsupervised clustering specifically because self-reported/task-assigned emotional-state labels were considered unreliable, and found this approach improved average recognition accuracy by roughly five percentage points over a purely supervised baseline — a close structural analog to your situation, since your five labels are also task-assignment proxies rather than verified internal states.

**Small-sample-robust hybrid clustering-classification.** A locally-linear-embedding + support-vector-clustering + support-vector-data-description (LLE-SVC-SVDD) framework was built specifically to handle mental-workload-level identification with small-sized training samples, using unsupervised clustering to first identify the "clear-cut" separable cases before a supervised stage handles the harder, overlapping cases — a reasonable template for handling your ~25-subject constraint without abandoning supervised learning entirely.

**Subject-level personalization / twin-archetype seeding.** This is the direct bridge to Section 2: rather than training one digital twin per subject from scratch (data-starved with 25 subjects, ~1,500 sub-windows each on average), cluster subjects by their baseline EEG/behavioral response profiles first, and initialize each subject's twin from their cluster's shared prior rather than from a generic population prior or from nothing. This borrows statistical strength across subjects who look alike while still allowing per-subject fine-tuning — conceptually the same move as the LLE-SVC-SVDD small-sample handling above, applied one level up (subjects rather than windows).

## 4. A methodological flag specific to your exact numbers

This wasn't something I went looking for — it surfaced directly from your stated numbers and is worth stating plainly because it will affect how any of these three techniques should be evaluated. You report 37,804 sliding 1-second sub-windows drawn from 2,908 four-second trials, under subject-dependent evaluation. Subject-dependent (segment-based) evaluation pools all subjects' data, segments it, and randomly splits segments into train/test — meaning segments from the same subject, and potentially the same trial, can appear on both sides of the split. This matters because segments from one subject are measurably more similar to each other than to segments from different subjects, and more specifically, randomly shuffling segments across trials before splitting can place highly-correlated *adjacent* segments from the same trial into both training and test sets, inflating measured accuracy relative to real-world deployment where such overlap won't occur. The empirical scale of this effect is not small: some studies see accuracy drop from above 95% under segment-based (leaky) evaluation to around 60% under clean subject-based holdout, and more broadly a survey of the translational EEG-deep-learning literature found the majority of studies use segment-based holdout and therefore likely overestimate real-world classification performance.

With 4-second trials sliced into overlapping-or-adjacent 1-second sub-windows, you have exactly the geometry this literature warns about — sub-windows from the same trial are highly autocorrelated (they share most of the same underlying cognitive episode), so if your current 73.34% LightGBM baseline used a random split at the sub-window level rather than a split enforced at the trial or subject level, that number may be inflated relative to genuine held-out generalization. This isn't a criticism of the number itself — it's a request worth making of whoever ran that evaluation, since it changes how any of scalogram-stacking, clustering, or digital-twin results should be benchmarked against it. Concretely: TSception addressed this by splitting cross-validation folds at the trial level specifically so that adjacent within-trial segments could never appear split across train and test, and the standard subject-independent alternative (leave-one-subject-out, or leave-k-subjects-out) enforces a hard constraint that the training-subject set and test-subject set be fully disjoint. I'd recommend reporting all three going forward: subject-dependent-with-trial-level-splitting, subject-dependent-with-window-level-splitting (to quantify the leakage gap directly), and leave-subject-out — especially since scalogram-CNN and digital-twin architectures are exactly the higher-capacity, higher-overfitting-risk model classes where this gap tends to be largest.
