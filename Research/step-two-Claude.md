## Gap 1 — Evidential Deep Learning & Uncertainty-Aware Classification

**Key finding**

EDL (Sensoy, Kaplan & Kandemir, NeurIPS 2018) replaces the softmax head with a non-negative "evidence" head (ReLU/softplus instead of softmax), and interprets that evidence as the parameters of a Dirichlet distribution over the class-probability simplex rather than a single point estimate. The network's predictions are treated as subjective opinions, and it learns to collect evidence for those opinions directly from data. Concretely, for K classes the network outputs evidence e = [e₁,…,e_K], e_k ≥ 0; the Dirichlet parameters are α_k = e_k + 1; the Dirichlet strength is S = Σα_k; the expected class probability is p̂_k = α_k/S; and the "vacuity" / uncertainty mass is u = K/S — u=1 when the network has collected zero evidence for every class (uniform Dirichlet, "I don't know"), and shrinks toward 0 as evidence accumulates.

**Loss function, mathematically:** standard cross-entropy is L_CE = −Σ y_k log(p_k) on a softmax point estimate. EDL instead uses a Bayes-risk (sum-of-squares) loss over the Dirichlet mean:

L_i = Σ_k (y_ik − p̂_ik)² + p̂_ik(1−p̂_ik)/(S_i+1)

— an error term plus an explicit *variance* term, so the network can't reduce loss just by pushing p̂ toward y; it also has to justify that confidence with evidence, which is what gives you calibration for free rather than as a post-hoc patch. This is regularized by a KL-divergence term that pulls evidence for the *non*-ground-truth classes back toward the uniform ("I-don't-know") Dirichlet:

L_total = L_i + λ_t · KL[D(p | α̃_i) ‖ D(p | ⟨1,…,1⟩)], where α̃ = y + (1−y)⊙α

with an annealing coefficient λ_t (typically ramped from 0 to 1 over the first ~10 epochs) so the network first learns to classify, then learns to be appropriately unsure. This decomposition — where the KL term shrinks total evidence toward zero for samples the network can't correctly classify — is exactly what drives the improved uncertainty behavior relative to plain cross-entropy.

**EDL applied to EEG specifically:** it's used, but not yet common, and the closest hit is diagnostic rather than cognitive-profiling. A 2025 framework (TRUEE) applies subjective-logic/EDL-style evidential networks to clinical EEG by decomposing the signal into temporal, spectral, and time-frequency "views," with each view producing an evidence vector that's mapped to Dirichlet parameters yielding a subjective opinion with explicit belief and uncertainty mass. Reported performance was 86.5% accuracy with 0.175 expected calibration error on ASD detection — but notably, the same paper argues single-view EDL is insufficient for clinical EEG precisely because EEG biomarkers manifest differently across representations, motivating their multi-view fusion instead of raw EDL. That's a direct, relevant caution for your setup: a single evidential head on top of one feature representation is likely to under-perform a multi-view or ensembled variant on 14-channel EEG.

**Alternatives for your 37K-sample / 5-class / 25-subject problem, compared honestly:**

- **MC-Dropout** (Gal & Ghahramani, ICML 2016): cheapest — reuses your existing net, just keep dropout active at inference and average multiple stochastic forward passes. Weakest uncertainty quality of the group; sensitive to dropout placement/rate.
- **Deep Ensembles** (Lakshminarayanan et al., NeurIPS 2017): costs N× training compute (e.g., 5 independently-initialized models) but is well worth it at your scale. In a head-to-head EEG comparison (Normal/MCI/Dementia classification), deep ensembles consistently outperformed other uncertainty methods on out-of-distribution and shifted EEG data, and model uncertainty rose appropriately under noise, drift, and frequency perturbation — this is a strong, directly-EEG precedent. Separately, deep ensembles have been shown to meaningfully improve both prediction and uncertainty estimation specifically in EEG analysis (Tveter et al.).
- **Bayesian Neural Networks** (full variational): theoretically the "right" answer, practically the hardest to get working well — mean-field approximations routinely under-estimate uncertainty, and you're unlikely to see it pay off relative to ensembles at this data scale.
- **EDL**: cheapest at inference (one network, one pass), and gives you a clean closed-form uncertainty mass — attractive for a live "here's your confidence" UI. The catch is the open theoretical question below.
- **Temperature Scaling** (Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger, ICML 2017): not a competitor to the above — it's a one-parameter post-hoc *recalibration* of the softmax max-confidence, fit on a held-out set. Guo et al. showed modern networks are systematically overconfident, and that this single-parameter rescaling is often surprisingly effective at fixing that overconfidence without touching accuracy. It doesn't decompose aleatoric/epistemic and won't help OOD detection — but you should run it as a baseline/sanity-check regardless of which of the above you pick, and report ECE before and after.

**Aleatoric vs. epistemic, and how to report them:** the standard framing (Hüllermeier & Waegeman, Machine Learning 2021) is that aleatoric uncertainty is irreducible noise in the data-generating process, while epistemic uncertainty reflects the model's own lack of knowledge and is in principle reducible with more data. For your system, aleatoric uncertainty is genuine trial-to-trial EEG noise or momentary lapses in task engagement — it won't shrink no matter how many people you add. Epistemic uncertainty is "have I seen a person with a brain-signal profile like this before" — with 25 subjects, this is almost certainly your dominant source of uncertainty, and it *should* shrink as you add subjects. These need to be surfaced differently to an end user: high aleatoric uncertainty on a segment should read as "this reading was noisy, consider re-testing"; high epistemic uncertainty should read as a system-level trust flag — "this tool hasn't seen enough people like you to be confident" — not as a statement about the person's cognition. Collapsing the two (e.g., reporting only total entropy) risks quietly relabeling "our training set was too narrow" as "your brain signal was ambiguous," which is a bad look in a career-recommendation context.

**Citations**

- Wu, [authors] et al. (2025). *Reliable decision making on clinical EEG: Trusted multi-view learning with subjective logic for uncertainty quantification*. (TRUEE framework) — Expert Systems with Applications / ScienceDirect.

**Practical implication for your system**
Given 25 subjects, don't trust random epoch-wise train/test splits for either accuracy *or* uncertainty reporting — epochs from the same person will leak across the split and your calibration will look better than it is. Use subject-wise (leave-N-subjects-out) cross-validation as your primary evaluation. Architecturally: deep ensembles (4–5 members) as your main uncertainty engine, with an EDL/Dirichlet head as a cheap secondary signal for the live per-user confidence display, and mandatory temperature-scaling + ECE reporting on top of both.

**Open debate to acknowledge**
Recent theoretical work argues standard EDL training objectives yield epistemic uncertainty that doesn't vanish even in the limit of infinite data — a violation of basic Bayesian consistency — and that EDL's "evidential signal" correlates more with how hard a sample is to classify (aleatoric) than with genuine gaps in model knowledge (epistemic). If you use EDL, you should not claim clean aleatoric/epistemic separation without caveating this critique — it's a live, unresolved dispute, not a settled implementation detail.

## Gap 2 — Neuroscience-Validated Cognitive Aptitude Mapping

**Key finding**

Four of your five classes map reasonably cleanly onto Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory, the dominant psychometric taxonomy of cognitive ability. CHC is a three-stratum hierarchy — general intelligence (g) at the top, roughly ten broad abilities below it including fluid reasoning (Gf), comprehension-knowledge (Gc), visual processing (Gv), working memory (Gwm), and processing speed (Gs), and 70+ narrow abilities at the base. Mental Arithmetic ≈ Gq/quantitative reasoning; Pattern Recognition ≈ Gv/Gf; Working Memory Retrieval ≈ Gwm (a named broad ability); Reading Comprehension ≈ Grw. **Sustained Attention is the odd one out** — it is not a CHC broad ability at all. The closest CHC-adjacent home is processing speed (Gs): Kevin McGrew, a CHC co-author, has specifically discussed the WJ-III Pair Cancellation test — a sustained-attention/vigilance measure — as loading onto the processing-speed factor rather than forming its own cluster, describing it as "an aspect of executive functioning" that the battery under-utilizes. The broader vigilance literature treats it as its own construct, studied through Continuous Performance Tests rather than ability batteries — the neuroscience literature explicitly distinguishes sustained attention/vigilance from the arousal and cognitive-ability constructs it's often conflated with in EEG interpretation. You'll want to name this mismatch directly rather than imply all five classes have equal psychometric footing.

GATB (the historical U.S. Department of Labor battery, predecessor to O*NET) is the classic "does decomposed ability predict job performance" precedent, and it's a cautionary one as much as a supportive one. Meta-analytic work confirms general mental ability (GMA) is one of the most consistently valid predictors of job performance and training success across occupational criteria. The catch: GATB's own internal dimensionality analysis found that a single general-ability factor accounts for most of the predictive variance, with specific aptitude factors and error variance making up the rest — i.e., the *general* factor does most of the work, and the *profile* (which specific aptitude is high vs. low) adds comparatively little. That's a direct structural tension with your project's whole framing, since "calibrated profile across 5 abilities" is explicitly betting that the shape of the profile matters, not just the overall level. GATB also carries real historical baggage worth naming: it was extensively used to study the ability–job-performance relationship, but from 1981 the U.S. Employment Service began "race-norming" its results, and a National Academy of Sciences review found reported validity coefficients in the r = .25–.35 range along with test-bias concerns — a direct precedent for exactly the fairness failure mode a modern neuro-aptitude tool needs to design against from day one, not retrofit later.

O*NET (the modern successor) is closer to what Gap 3 asks about, but note what it actually is: it rates 52 abilities spanning cognitive, physical, psychomotor, and sensory domains — including oral comprehension and number facility — with importance/level scores for 900+ occupations, and its cognitive-ability list includes items like Deductive Reasoning, Inductive Reasoning, Mathematical Reasoning, Memorization, Selective Attention, Time Sharing, and Written/Oral Comprehension. This is a descriptive taxonomy (job analysts rating what occupations *require*), not a validated predictive model of who will succeed at them — an important distinction for how you frame any lookup against it (more in Gap 3).

**EEG → career-performance link:** genuinely thin, and this is the crux of the paper's credibility. One directly relevant study classifies levels of executive function from resting-state EEG specifically for executive-recruitment support, explicitly noting that prior EEG-performance work almost entirely targets intra-subject, single-task prediction rather than the inter-subject, cross-task generalization recruitment would need. A separate resting-state connectivity paper makes the same point from the other direction: most EEG-based trait/performance prediction targets one task at a time, and the omission of a multi-task, personnel-assessment-oriented approach from the literature is called out by the authors themselves as surprising given the potential impact. Net read: there is no published, replicated chain running from "EEG cognitive-state classifier" to "predicts real-world career or job performance." What exists is (a) EEG-workload/vigilance monitoring *during* a task (mature — see Gap 4), and (b) a handful of early, single-study EEG-recruitment proofs of concept. Your paper would be extending (b), not confirming an established result.

**Legal/ethical framework, current as of today (this moved recently — worth verifying again close to publication):**

*US:* The EEOC issued a December 2024 fact sheet, "Wearables in the Workplace," specifically addressing devices that track brain activity alongside location and heart rate, under federal nondiscrimination law. This sits on top of EEOC's broader 2022 ADA technical guidance warning that algorithmic/AI assessment tools can screen out people with disabilities even unintentionally, and that this can also constitute a prohibited disability-related medical inquiry — a live concern for a cognitive profile, since a low working-memory or attention score is plausibly correlated with an undisclosed condition (ADHD, learning disability). This isn't hypothetical: a federal court has already allowed a discrimination suit to proceed against an AI hiring-assessment vendor (Mobley v. Workday), and EEOC's own data shows disability-discrimination charges tied to neurodivergence rising sharply in recent years — a neuro-aptitude tool sits squarely in the path of that trend.

*EU:* the AI Act status is time-sensitive, so check it again before submission. As of today: the Act has applied in stages since entering into force August 2024 — prohibited practices since February 2025, general-purpose-AI rules since August 2025 — and a Digital Omnibus (in force since 27 July 2026) has just postponed the high-risk employment-AI obligations (Annex III, which explicitly covers recruitment, screening, and performance-prediction systems) from the original August 2026 date to December 2027. So the full high-risk regime for employment AI is *not yet* legally binding in the EU as of today, but you should design to it now since it's the clear target. Separately and already in force: Article 5 bans emotion recognition in the workplace outright (narrow medical/safety exceptions only) and bans biometric categorization revealing sensitive traits. A cognitive-state classifier isn't emotion recognition, but the line is close enough that legal review should treat it as adjacent rather than clearly exempt, especially if outputs could function as a proxy for protected characteristics.

**Practical implication for your system**
Don't present the 5-class map as a validated psychometric battery — frame it explicitly as "EEG-classifiable task states inspired by CHC-adjacent constructs," name the specific broad ability each class approximates, and flag Sustained Attention as structurally different from the other four (it's a vigilance/executive-function construct, not a CHC ability). Treat every output as sensitive/quasi-medical data by default regardless of your stated purpose — build an accommodation/appeal pathway before you build a leaderboard. Don't position this for EU employment deployment before December 2027, and design the whole pipeline (documentation, human oversight, bias testing) as if the high-risk obligations already applied.

**Open debate to acknowledge**
GATB's own finding — that a general factor swamps the specific-aptitude profile in predictive value — is a direct challenge to the premise that a 5-way *profile* (versus a single overall confidence/competence score) is where the useful signal lives. Your paper should address this head-on rather than assume the profile framing is self-evidently better.

## Gap 3 — Probabilistic Cognitive-to-Career Mapping

**Key finding**

Worth separating up front: RIASEC/O*NET Interest Profiler measures *interest* (what you like), not *ability* (what you're good at) — a different construct from what your EEG classifier measures, and one you shouldn't casually merge with it. Its predictive validity, even after 25+ years of validation, sets a useful realism check: a meta-analysis of 53 studies found congruence–satisfaction correlations around r = .17 (rising to ~.25 under better measurement), and a separate meta-analysis found interests predict job performance at roughly r = .23. If a mature, extensively-validated instrument tops out around r = .2–.3, that's the realistic ceiling to set expectations against for a first-generation EEG-derived mapping — not 90%+ "accuracy," which is a different (and easier) statistical claim than career-outcome validity. Independently, a confirmatory factor analysis of the O*NET Interest Profiler Short Form found poor fit to its own claimed six-factor RIASEC structure — even the structural side isn't unanimous.

For your specific "is there a lookup table" question: O*NET's Abilities database (52 abilities × 900+ occupations, Importance/Level ratings — see Gap 2) is the closest thing that exists, and it's descriptive, not predictive — built from job analysts rating requirements, not from validated data on who actually succeeds. Using it as your target space is reasonable; presenting a match against it as validated career-fit prediction would overstate what the underlying data supports.

On technique: your four options aren't equally supported in the literature.
- **Rule-based thresholding** has no dedicated literature to cite because it's not really a research technique — but it's arguably the most defensible *first version* precisely because it doesn't manufacture false precision from a 73%-accuracy, 25-subject classifier.
- **Fuzzy logic** — I did not find a body of work specifically applying fuzzy-logic mapping to cognitive-to-career prediction; the fuzzy-deep-learning literature I found is about uncertainty in EEG signal classification itself, not the downstream career-mapping layer. I'd flag this as an under-explored option rather than a validated one.
- **A learned MLP/ANN mapping** has real precedent — one recent system trains an MLP on academic/extracurricular data to predict career paths, reporting 94.71% validation accuracy, and another using Random Forest, Decision Tree, SVM, and ANN reports up to 91.4% accuracy predicting career domains from personality, aptitude, and academic data. Read these numbers skeptically: they're near-uniformly self-reported on each team's own small, idiosyncratic labeled set, with no external replication or transfer to a different population — a pattern worth flagging as a field-wide issue, not a green light to expect similar numbers from your own held-out data.
- **Bayesian inference over O*NET data** has the strongest direct precedent, and it's O*NET's *own* methodology: in developing its Occupational Interest Profiles, O*NET's own analysts' RIASEC ratings were converted to posterior probabilities per occupation, with the top-probability category exceeding .99 in over 79% of occupational units. This is a genuine structural argument for treating your EEG classifier's Dirichlet/evidence output as a prior or likelihood term combined with O*NET's per-occupation ability profiles via a graphical/Bayesian model, rather than collapsing to point estimates and doing nearest-neighbor matching.

**How existing systems actually build the mapping layer, concretely:** Pymetrics is the most directly comparable production system, and its methodology is well-documented — it uses ~16 short games to extract traits like risk propensity, working memory, and planning, then trains a separate binary classifier per client/role, contrasting the gameplay of that company's top performers against a reference population, followed by an unsupervised clustering step that builds the "target profile" for each role, then statistical fairness comparisons across demographic groups before deployment — architecturally, a strong analogy for "learn the target occupation's profile empirically, then score new people against it," which is more defensible than a fixed lookup table if you have the data to support it.

**Uncertainty propagation from EEG layer to career layer** — this is exactly the part of your pipeline design most likely to silently fail if unaddressed. If your EEG classifier outputs a near-uniform, high-vacuity Dirichlet for a given user, and your career-mapping layer just takes the argmax/point estimate forward, you'll launder Layer-1 uncertainty into a confident-looking Layer-2 recommendation — the single most important bug to design against. Prior work on cascaded medical-imaging pipelines shows that explicitly propagating uncertainty estimates through each stage (rather than passing only point predictions) measurably improves downstream inference quality, and a directly analogous EEG pipeline (U-PASS, for sleep staging) that folds uncertainty estimation into every stage — data selection, training, and deployment — improved expert-level accuracy from a 75% baseline to 85%. Given your EDL/Dirichlet output is already a subjective-logic representation, the natural path is Dempster-Shafer-style belief combination into the career layer rather than a separate propagation scheme.

**Citations**
- Nye, C.D., Su, R., Rounds, J., & Drasgow, F. (2012). *Vocational interests and performance: A quantitative summary of over 60 years of research*. Perspectives on Psychological Science.
- Rounds, J., et al. *Development of Occupational Interest Profiles for O\*NET* (O\*NET Center technical report) — Bayesian posterior-probability methodology.
- Wilson, C., & Caliskan, A. (2021). *Building and Auditing Fair Algorithms: A Case Study in Candidate Screening*. FAccT '21 (the Pymetrics audit case study).

**Practical implication for your system**
State plainly in the paper that no validated cognitive-ability-to-occupation lookup table exists — O*NET is descriptive, not predictive, so a match against it is a *plausibility* signal, not a validated fit score. Architecturally: treat the EEG classifier's Dirichlet output as evidence to be Bayesian-combined with O*NET occupational-ability profiles (leaning on the O*NET OIP posterior-probability precedent), not as a point estimate to be nearest-neighbor matched, and make sure a low-confidence EEG read visibly widens/flattens the resulting career distribution rather than being silently dropped.

**Open debate to acknowledge**
Recent auditing of a large-scale production Pymetrics dataset (2018–2022) suggests adverse impact can persist even after formal bias auditing, plausibly because "behavioral" trait features still function as unintended proxies for protected characteristics — directly relevant, since EEG-derived cognitive features are at least as plausible a proxy channel (for SES, education, neurodivergence) as game-behavior features, and "de-biasing" your labels doesn't guarantee your neural features are clean.

## Gap 4 — Validation of End-to-End Neural Aptitude Systems

**Key finding**

The field draws a sharper line than your prompt implies between two very different validation problems, and it matters which one you're claiming: **(a) EEG-based *state* monitoring during a task** (mature, well-precedented) versus **(b) EEG-based *trait* prediction of aptitude for a not-yet-attempted career** (immature, few precedents, and even those show only moderate effect sizes).

For (a): EEG is described as a promising tool for pilot selection, training, and real-time competency monitoring, with resting-state EEG indicators developed specifically for pilot competency assessment, and live-flight EEG data from actual student pilots has been used to discriminate cognitive workload during flight with machine learning — this is a real, replicated, if narrow, body of work. But notice what's actually being validated: moment-to-moment workload/vigilance *during the task itself*, not "will this untested person make a good pilot."

For (b), the closest real analogue is BCI-aptitude prediction — literally predicting how well someone will learn to operate a brain-computer interface from their baseline EEG — and it's instructive precisely because it's a narrower, more mechanistically-motivated question than career aptitude, and still only moderately successful. Auditory-oddball ERP features were found to moderately predict aptitude for an auditory P300-BCI and more strongly for a visual P300-BCI, in a controlled 40-participant study, and structural brain measures (white-matter fractional anisotropy in specific tracts) correlated with individual BCI accuracy at r = 0.51–0.63 — meaningful, publishable correlations, but nowhere near deterministic, for a question that's mechanistically much closer to the measured signal than "vocational fit" is.

**Gold-standard comparisons, and what "validate" should mean here:** the field uses (i) convergent validity against established paper-and-pencil measures of the same construct (correlate your "arithmetic" class confidence against an actual arithmetic-reasoning test score) — cheapest, fastest, and the single most convincing thing you could add to this paper; (ii) real downstream outcomes (grades, performance ratings) — expensive, slow, and even legacy instruments (GATB, RIASEC) only achieve r ≈ .2–.3 against this bar, so don't over-promise it for a first paper; (iii) expert panel review — more common in clinical neuropsych validation than vocational contexts, less applicable here.

**Statistical measures beyond accuracy:** psychometric practice separates reliability (test-retest stability, internal consistency across items) from validity (does the score reflect the construct it claims to, typically via convergent/discriminant correlations and factor analysis) as two distinct, both-necessary properties — modern usage collapses the older face/content/criterion distinctions into a single unified "construct validity," defined as the degree to which a score can be legitimately interpreted as representing the underlying construct. On the calibration side, report Expected Calibration Error and reliability diagrams alongside accuracy (per Guo et al., Gap 1) — accuracy alone tells a reader nothing about whether your confidence numbers mean anything.

**Citations**
- Halder, S., et al. (2013). *Prediction of Auditory and Visual P300 Brain-Computer Interface Aptitude*. (PMC3573031)
- Frontiers in Human Neuroscience. *Prediction of brain-computer interface aptitude from individual brain structure*.
- Yun, D., et al. *Evaluating General-Purpose AI with Psychometrics* — useful as a cross-domain analogy for treating reliability and construct validity as first-class evaluation criteria, not just accuracy.
- Kraemer, H.C., et al. *Current Concepts in Validity and Reliability for Psychometric Instruments: Theory and Application*. American Journal of Medicine.

**Practical implication for your system**
Before anything else, run subject-wise test-retest reliability on your existing 25 subjects (same person, different day/session — does the Dirichlet profile correlate?). If this fails, no amount of downstream career-mapping sophistication matters, and it's the cheapest possible check to run first. Then add convergent validity against one established paper-and-pencil test per class — this alone would be the strongest evidence in the paper. Report ECE/reliability diagrams alongside accuracy throughout. And be explicit in the paper that career-*outcome* validity is out of scope for this phase — name it as required future work rather than letting the state-monitoring literature's maturity lend implicit credibility to the trait-prediction claim, which is a different and much less validated thing.

**Open debate to acknowledge**
The EEG-workload/vigilance-monitoring literature is genuinely mature (aviation, driving, cognitive-load contexts), and it would be easy for a reader — or a reviewer — to let that maturity bleed into an impression that trait-like vocational aptitude prediction is similarly well-established. It isn't. Even the mechanistically-narrower BCI-aptitude-prediction literature, which is about as close an analogue as exists, tops out at moderate correlations. Naming this distinction explicitly in your limitations section will read as more credible than eliding it, since a knowledgeable reviewer will notice either way.

Nothing above resolves that; it's worth either treating it as the paper's honestly-flagged central limitation, or considering a narrower reframing (e.g., a cognitive-engagement/self-awareness feedback tool rather than a career-recommendation engine) that keeps the interesting technical contributions while sidestepping a lot of the validity and legal exposure discussed in Gaps 2 and 4.
