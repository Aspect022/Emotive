# Gap 1: Evidential Deep Learning & Uncertainty in Small EEG Classification

**Key finding:** Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) replaces the usual softmax output with a Dirichlet-distribution output, enabling the network to express *uncertainty* along with class probabilities.  Instead of outputting a point estimate \(p(y|x)\), an EDL network produces evidence values \(\{e_k\}\) for each class, forming Dirichlet parameters \(\alpha_k = e_k + 1\).  The expected class probabilities are \( \hat p_k = \alpha_k/\sum_j\alpha_j\), and the **uncertainty (vacuity)** can be quantified by \(u = K / \sum_j\alpha_j\) (with \(K\) classes).  In practice the loss changes from standard cross-entropy to an “evidential” loss that penalizes both prediction error and uncertainty.  For example, Sensoy *et al.* (2018) use a **squared-error + variance** term: 
\[
\mathcal L_i = \sum_{k=1}^K\Big[(y_{ik} - \hat p_{ik})^2 + \frac{\hat p_{ik}(1-\hat p_{ik})}{S_i+1}\Big] \;+\; \lambda\,\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_i)\,\|\,\mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf{1}))\,,
\]
where \(S_i=\sum_j\alpha_{ij}\).  The first term is prediction error + uncertainty (the “Bayes risk”), and the KL regularizer drives the Dirichlet toward a flat prior when evidence is low.  Thus an EDL model explicitly quantifies **epistemic uncertainty** (through the Dirichlet concentration) while data noise contributes to **aleatoric uncertainty** (the variance term). In short, replacing softmax with Dirichlet permits principled uncertainty estimates: if the network sees *no* evidence, \(\alpha\approx 1\) and all \(\hat p\approx 1/K\) with high uncertainty; if it is confident, one \(\alpha_k\) dominates and uncertainty is low.

**Application to EEG/BCI:**  In biosignal tasks, EDL is still nascent.  A recent review finds that EDL “has shown good performance on out-of-distribution detection” but with “limitations when representing epistemic uncertainty”.  In EEG specifically, most uncertainty-aware work has focused on simpler methods.  For example, Tveter *et al.* (2026) benchmarked **Deep Ensembles** vs. **MC-Dropout** on EEG classification and found ensembles far outperform dropout: ensembles gave “more informative and reliable uncertainty estimates” under dataset shifts.  **Bayesian neural nets (BNNs)** can quantify both uncertainties but are complex and hard to scale in practice.  **Temperature Scaling** (TS) is a lightweight post-hoc calibration of softmax outputs (Guo *et al.*, 2017) but only corrects confidence, not underlying model uncertainty.  

For a 37K-sample, 5-class EEG problem, **Deep Ensembles** are likely state-of-art for epistemic uncertainty (at the cost of training/ensembling multiple models).  MC-Dropout is easier to implement (no retraining) but requires many forward passes and often underestimates uncertainty. BNNs (e.g. variational or Hamiltonian methods) give full Bayesian treatment but are computationally heavy and may overfit on small data. Temperature scaling could calibrate the final probabilities but *cannot* express uncertainty due to data scarcity.  **Practical implication:** we should experiment with ensembles and evidential nets.  For instance, an Evidential NN might give a Dirichlet-based profile of aptitudes (with a concentration parameter indicating confidence).  But Tveter *et al.* suggest ensembles provide the most robust uncertainty. We should also explicitly evaluate *calibration* (see below).  

**Aleatoric vs. epistemic:**  In EEG, *aleatoric* uncertainty arises from inherent noise in brain signals (e.g. electrode noise, intrinsic neural variability) and is irreducible.  In contrast, *epistemic* uncertainty comes from model insufficiency (e.g. limited training data or mismatched domains).  For a career profiling system, aleatoric uncertainty means “the signal itself is noisy or ambiguous” – so even repeated tests by the same person might fluctuate. Epistemic uncertainty means “our model isn’t sure due to lack of data or novelty”, suggesting we might reduce uncertainty by more data or by reporting lower confidence.  We should report both: e.g. showing confidence intervals on each aptitude score.  If uncertainty is mostly epistemic, we might flag the result as preliminary and suggest retesting; if aleatoric, it indicates innate fluctuation in that task.

**Open debates:**  EDL is still under scrutiny.  Some researchers question whether current EDL losses truly disentangle epistemic vs aleatoric uncertainty.  In small-sample biosignals, it’s unclear if Dirichlet-based methods outperform simpler approximations.  We should acknowledge that no uncertainty method is perfect: ensemble and dropout methods also have trade-offs.  Moreover, calibration remains critical – even an evidential model can be overconfident. Recent work recommends evaluating Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Maximum Calibration Error, not just accuracy.  

**Citations:** Sensoy *et al.* 2018 (NeurIPS) on Evidential DL;  Tveter *et al.* 2026 (Artificial Intelligence in Medicine) on EEG uncertainty and ensembles; Van Gorp *et al.* 2026 (J. Healthcare Inf. Res.) on UQ methods for biosignals.

**Practical implication:**  In our system, replace the softmax classifier with an evidential network that outputs Dirichlet parameters. Use the evidential loss (squared-error + variance + KL) instead of cross-entropy. Also calibrate and validate its confidence (compute ECE). For comparison, we should implement a small ensemble of 3–5 classifiers (to keep cost manageable) and evaluate uncertainty: the literature suggests ensembles yield more reliable epistemic uncertainty. Finally, decompose total uncertainty into aleatoric vs. epistemic – for example, high model uncertainty might trigger a re-test.

**Controversies:**  There is debate on how to correctly quantify epistemic uncertainty with EDL vs. Bayesian methods.  Also, some argue that neural networks can never be perfectly calibrated or that uncertainty estimates may give a false sense of reliability. We should note these caveats and stress that our cognitive profiles will include confidence measures, not absolute truth.  

# Gap 2: Neuroscience-Validated Cognitive–Career Mapping

**Key finding:**  There is **no single gold-standard neuroscience map** from EEG-measured tasks to careers.  However, established psychometric frameworks do link cognitive abilities to job requirements. For example, the *O*NET Content Model* (US Dept. of Labor) and the older GATB/ASVAB tests define specific abilities (e.g. Mathematical Reasoning, Written Comprehension, Spatial Visualization, Selective Attention) that correspond closely to our EEG classes.  Kato & Scherbaum (2023) explicitly align the GATB aptitudes to O*NET abilities: e.g. **“Numerical aptitude” → “Mathematical reasoning”/“Number facility”, “Verbal aptitude” → “Written comprehension”, “Spatial aptitude” → “Visualization”**, etc..  By extension, mental arithmetic maps to quantitative reasoning, pattern recognition to spatial visualization, working memory to memory (recall) tasks, reading comprehension to verbal knowledge, and sustained attention to selective/​vigilance abilities in O*NET. The Cattell–Horn–Carroll (CHC) model similarly treats fluid reasoning (Gf), visual processing (Gv), working memory (Gsm/Gwm), crystallized knowledge (Gc) and processing speed/attention (Gs) as broad factors underlying such tasks.

Occupational theories like Holland’s RIASEC classify interests (not abilities) into six types (Realistic, Investigative, etc.), which is complementary but not directly about aptitude. O*NET’s Interest Profiler (RIASEC) and *career clusters* are largely interest/personality driven, not cognitive. By contrast, ability-based placement systems (ASVAB in military, GATB historically) use quantitative and verbal scores to suggest career clusters. For instance, ASVAB composite scores correlate with job zones (e.g. STEM‐type roles need high quantitative and spatial scores). 

In practice, one can use O*NET’s database of ability importance: each occupation has ratings on the same abilities (math, pattern, memory, attention, etc.).  Thus a lookup table is effectively the O*NET ability profiles. For example, Kato *et al.* compute “job tilt” scores by differencing O*NET-rated abilities.  **However, there is scant direct research linking EEG-derived scores to career outcomes.**  We found no published studies using EEG to predict vocational success. One related domain is pilot screening: EEG is used to detect neurological issues, but not as a cognitive predictor.  A few neuroergonomics studies infer cognitive load from EEG for safety, but not career aptitude. In short, there is no evidence that EEG-based cognitive profiles have validated links to long-term job performance or satisfaction. 

**Ethical/Legal:** In the US, employment testing must comply with the **Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)** and EEOC guidelines. EEG data likely count as medical information: the ADA defines a “medical examination” broadly (any test revealing mental/physical impairment).  Employers cannot require such tests pre-offer unless strictly job-related.  Using brain data to screen applicants risks violating ADA (medical exam before employment) and the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA) if seen as health data.  Even if technically legal, using neural data for hiring would be an EEOC red flag if it has disparate impact. 

In the EU, the new AI Act (Reg. 2024/1689) classifies “AI used for recruitment or selection” as **high-risk**. This imposes requirements for transparency, fairness, bias monitoring, and human oversight. Brain data are even more sensitive (potentially “biometric” or health data), so any AI profiling here must meet strict consent and data protection (GDPR) standards.  We must acknowledge that *any* neurological hiring tool is ethically fraught: it implicates privacy, informed consent, and the risk of stigmatizing candidates.  

**Open debates:**  Psychometricians debate how much specific abilities add beyond general intelligence (g) for job fit.  Kato & Scherbaum find only modest incremental validity of “ability tilts” over g for job performance (mean \(R^2\) ~7%).  Others caution that aptitude tests can be biased or culturally loaded.  Neurologists warn that EEG is noisy and may reflect state (fatigue, stress) more than trait aptitude.  Legally, critics argue that brain-based selection could violate human rights (the “right to cognitive privacy”) and EEOC’s anti-discrimination norms. Our system must therefore stress that EEG-profiles are *supplemental* to standard assessments, not sole evidence of aptitude, and must preserve confidentiality and non-discrimination.

**Citations:** Kato & Scherbaum (2023), *Journal of Intelligence* – “Cognitive Ability Tilt and Job Performance” (mapping GATB↔O*NET); Newman & Isenhath (2010), *The Federal Lawyer* – ADA medical exam guidance; Ogletree (2025) – overview of EU AI Act on HR.

**Practical implication:** We should align our EEG tasks with established ability constructs. For reporting, translate EEG-derived “scores” into the language of recognized abilities (e.g. “Numerical Reasoning”, “Visual-Spatial”). We can leverage O*NET ability profiles: for each career cluster, compute aggregate requirements in these abilities (using O*NET data). Then map a user’s EEG-profile into those clusters. Legally, we must treat EEG as a sensitive test: obtain explicit consent, ensure it does not screen out protected groups, and provide fallback human review. We should also recommend cross-validation of any recommendation against conventional aptitude tests (e.g. ASVAB-like quizzes or GPA). 

**Controversies:**  We must note that using neural data for “aptitude” is hotly debated. Some neuroscientists argue no clear neural signature of job skills exists beyond general cognitive function. Others worry about neuro-determinism (believing EEG “dictates” career fate). Our paper should acknowledge that we are exploring a novel, unproven approach. We should cite the lack of prior evidence as a limitation, not oversell the claims, and mention that experts differ on the ethics of brain-based hiring.

# Gap 3: Probabilistic Cognitive-to-Career Mapping

**Key finding:**  Existing career guidance tools use a variety of approaches, but none exactly match our calibrated EEG profiles.  For example, the O*NET Interest Profiler (based on Holland’s RIASEC) maps *self-reported interests* to career clusters, not measured aptitudes. Pymetrics (a game-based assessment) and Cognify (a gamified cognitive test by Criteria Corp.) claim to map behavioral and cognitive traits to job recommendations, but their algorithms are proprietary and not peer-reviewed. There is no publicly available “lookup table” from cognitive test scores to O*NET job families. 

However, we can build on O*NET’s rich data. Each occupation in O*NET is rated for the importance/level of the same abilities we measure (math, spatial, memory, attention, etc.).  In principle, one could compute for each occupational family (e.g. STEM, social, creative) the typical profile of those five abilities. Our *mapping layer* could then use a **Bayesian inference** or **ML** approach: for each career cluster \(c\), compute \(P(c \mid \text{EEG profile}) \propto P(\text{profile}\mid c)P(c)\).  If we treat each ability score as a feature, a simple approach is a **multilayer perceptron** (MLP) or logistic regression trained on a labeled dataset of profiles→career.  In absence of such data, **rule-based** or **fuzzy logic** heuristics can approximate it: e.g., “if memory≫ others, favor research jobs; if attention≫ others, favor air-traffic control” (illustrative only).  

**Handling uncertainty:** Crucially, we should propagate uncertainty from the EEG layer through the recommendation. For instance, if the EEG Dirichlet is broad (high uncertainty), we can Monte-Carlo sample many cognitive profiles from that distribution and pass each through the mapping to get a distribution over careers. Or we can use a Bayesian network that takes a Dirichlet distribution over abilities as input, yielding a posterior over career clusters. In any case, the output should be probabilistic (e.g. “80% STEM, 15% creative, 5% other”) with confidence intervals. That way, an uncertain EEG result yields a diffuse career recommendation. 

**Open debate:**  There is debate over data-driven vs. rule-based career matching. Pure ML models risk encoding bias or spurious correlations if trained on limited data. On the other hand, rigid rules may not capture complex fit. Some recent work (e.g. Babak Majidi *et al.*, 2023) uses Bayesian networks for job recommendations, but typically from resumes or skills, not from cognitive tests. We should mention that our “mapping” is exploratory: we may need to refine it with occupational psychologists. Also, talent-matching startups often lack transparency, so reviewers may question how our profiles feed into suggestions. This argues for a clear, interpretable mapping (e.g. showing how each ability influenced the career scores) wherever possible.

**Citations:** Kato & Scherbaum (2023) discuss how cognitive abilities (from GATB) align with O*NET ability descriptors. (No direct academic sources were found for cognitive→career mapping; much of this design will be novel.) 

**Practical implication:** We will implement a two-step probabilistic system: (1) Take the EEG-derived cognitive profile (with Dirichlet uncertainty). (2) Use a learned mapping (e.g. Bayesian or neural network) to infer career probabilities.  We should ensure this second model can ingest uncertainty (e.g. via sampling or Bayesian layers).  If data are very limited, we might start with a fuzzy-rule prototype and iterate. The design must guarantee that if EEG is uncertain (flat Dirichlet), the career output remains cautious (e.g. label as “uncertain”). 

**Controversies:**  We should note that any career algorithm is somewhat arbitrary. There is no consensus taxonomy of “cognitive aptitudes → career clusters” in literature. Systems like Holland’s RIASEC focus on interests, not abilities. Another debate: should one use “hard thresholds” (“if math > 70% then STEM”) vs. continuous inference?  We lean toward a probabilistic (Bayesian) approach to honor uncertainty. Some experts also warn that psychological testing must consider test-taker motivation; EEG may be less malleable but still subject to fatigue effects. We should be transparent that our mapping is an initial model and will need empirical validation (see Gap 4).

# Gap 4: Validation of End-to-End Neural Aptitude Systems

**Key finding:**  Validating an EEG-to-career system is challenging and unprecedented.  There is no gold-standard “brain-based career recommendation” to compare against. The best we can do is compare our recommendations to established aptitude measures and real-world outcomes. Common benchmarks include: (a) **Standard cognitive/aptitude tests** (e.g. ASVAB, SAT/GRE, or specialized job samples), (b) **Academic records or performance metrics** (e.g. grades in math/language courses), or (c) **Expert evaluations** (career counselors judging profiles). Self-reported interest inventories (RIASEC) or Big-5 personality profiles could also serve as partial cross-checks, though they measure different constructs.

We should validate both the cognitive profiling and the career mapping separately. For the cognitive classifier, beyond accuracy we must assess *reliability* and *calibration*: for example, split-half reliability of EEG scores, test–retest stability across sessions, and expected calibration error (ECE) of the predicted probabilities. For the career recommendations, we need ground truth: this is hardest. One option is a **predictive validity study**: see if our suggested careers correlate with subjects’ actual education/profession or with their performance on domain-specific tasks. For instance, if someone’s profile says “Quantitative high”, do they indeed score high on math tests or succeed in technical tasks? 

**Examples of EEG/Neuro aptitude tools:**  There are very few precedents. One example is a recent pilot study by Binias *et al.* (2020): they used EEG to **predict pilots’ reaction times** and achieved a mean absolute error of ~114 ms. This shows EEG can forecast a specific cognitive performance metric relevant to job safety. Other work (e.g. EEG detection of workload in driving simulators) exists, but not framed as “aptitude assessment”. In clinical neuropsychology, EEG is used diagnostically (e.g. detecting minimal consciousness), but not for career fit. A classic note: many air forces *screen pilots’ EEGs* for epilepsy risk, but they explicitly caution it should never disqualify a candidate on its own. That literature emphasizes caution, which we should echo.

**Metrics beyond accuracy:** For this system we must report standard reliability and validity stats, not just classifier accuracy.  Key measures include: 
- **Calibration error (ECE/MCE):** how well predicted probabilities match observed frequencies. A low calibration error means our confidence scores are meaningful to humans. 
- **Brier score:** measures squared error of probabilistic predictions.
- **Test–retest reliability:** consistency of cognitive profile over repeated EEG sessions.
- **Construct validity:** correlation of EEG-based “memory score” with conventional memory test performance, etc.
- **Construct coherence:** e.g. does the network’s “pattern recognition” correlate with a standard visuo-spatial IQ measure?  
- **User studies:** Because this is decision-support, we might conduct a small human-subjects evaluation: have career counselors or participants rate how reasonable the suggestions seem.

**Open debates:**  Historically, aptitude testing emphasizes validity (can it predict job performance) and fairness. There is debate about using any biological measure for selection: critics call it neuro-mythmaking unless rigorously validated. Our statistical validation should therefore be especially rigorous: e.g. confidence intervals, blind testing, and transparency. Some will demand long-term longitudinal studies (which we likely cannot do) – we should acknowledge that. Finally, one controversy is how to handle errors: if we wrongly rank someone’s strengths, could that damage their prospects? We must discuss ethical safeguards, such as always including human judgment, not automating decisions purely on EEG.

**Citations:** Binias *et al.* (2020) *Frontiers in Neuroinformatics* – “Prediction of Pilot’s Reaction Time Based on EEG”; Van Gorp *et al.* (2026) – on uncertainty metrics (ECE/MCE) for bio-signals; Newman & Isenhath (2010) (for the ADA definition of “medical exam” via psychological tests, relevant to how such testing is regulated).

**Practical implication:**  In our evaluation, we will not stop at accuracy. We should compute calibration (e.g. reliability diagrams, ECE) and explicitly separate aleatoric vs. epistemic uncertainty (perhaps using held-out subjects as an “out-of-distribution” test).  We should pilot with a small cohort: have them take a standard aptitude test (or academic course grades) alongside our EEG protocol and correlate the results. A team of career counselors could review the profiles and rate recommendation quality. 

We must also comply with EEOC/EEG guidelines: for example, treat any EEG procedure as a *test*, and ensure any withdrawal (e.g. dropping candidates who “fail” EEG criteria) would be job-related and validated (which it is not). We should likely position this as purely advisory, not as a mandatory hiring filter, to stay safe legally.

**Controversies:**  We should note that any claims of predictive power are provisional. Even academic aptitude tests achieve only moderate validities (often 0.3–0.6 correlations with job success). There is debate whether additional signals (like “ability tilts” above general g) add meaningful prediction. We may find our EEG profile shows small correlations with outcome, and we should honestly report effect sizes and limitations. Skeptics will point out risks of overfitting or of conflating state (today’s brain state) with trait aptitude. We should discuss these as open issues and suggest further research (e.g. larger samples, different populations) to establish real-world validity.
